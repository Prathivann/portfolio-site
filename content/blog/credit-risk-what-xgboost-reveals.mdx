## The Standard Approach Falls Short

Most credit risk models in production still rely on logistic regression. There are good reasons for this — interpretability, regulatory comfort, decades of institutional familiarity. But logistic regression makes a strong assumption: that the relationship between each feature and the log-odds of default is linear and additive.

In consumer credit data, that assumption breaks down in predictable ways.

## Where Nonlinearity Lives

Consider utilization rate. A borrower at 30% utilization is meaningfully different from one at 70%, but the jump from 70% to 95% carries disproportionately more risk — the relationship is convex, not linear. Logistic regression can only approximate this by binning or adding polynomial terms manually. XGBoost finds it automatically.

The same pattern appears in payment history. A single missed payment in 36 months tells a very different story than three consecutive misses — but the *sequence* matters as much as the count. Tree-based models naturally capture these interaction effects without requiring the analyst to specify them upfront.

## The Experiment

For my credit risk segmentation project, I ran both approaches on the same preprocessed dataset:

- **Logistic regression** with standard scaling, no interaction terms
- **XGBoost** with 5-fold cross-validation, early stopping, and default hyperparameters

The XGBoost model identified a distinct "hidden risk" segment — borrowers with strong headline metrics (high income, low utilization) but subtle secondary signals (recent credit inquiries clustered in a short window, high revolving-to-installment ratio) that logistic regression scored as low risk.

## What This Means for Portfolio Decisions

This isn't an argument that XGBoost should replace logistic regression everywhere — it shouldn't, especially in regulated environments where model interpretability is a compliance requirement. But for internal analytics, screening, and portfolio-level risk monitoring, the ability to surface nonlinear interactions matters.

The practical implication: if your risk segmentation only uses linear models, you likely have a cluster of accounts that *look* safe by traditional metrics but carry elevated default probability that only shows up in interaction effects.

Statistical validation using Chi-squared tests and ANOVA confirmed that the segments XGBoost identified were significantly different from each other — this wasn't overfitting to noise, but real structure in the data.
